Entry 2.1 · Grade 11 · Bronze Award

Physics Bowl, Division II

American Association of Physics Teachers. Bronze Award, Division II.

Forty multiple-choice physics questions in forty-five minutes.

I wanted to challenge myself beyond the classroom. A friend and I stumbled on this competition and went together to our physics teacher to ask him to host it. He accepted the proposal and was happy to help us prepare.

Division II targets second-year physics students. I wanted to be more challenged, so I entered it as a first-year student. That meant studying the second-year IB physics material on my own. It was extra work, but I enjoyed connecting the new concepts to the old ones and building a better understanding of both.

Entry 2.2 · Grade 11 · 1st in School, 3rd in Korea

Sir Isaac Newton Exam

University of Waterloo. First in school, third in Korea.

Waterloo’s physics contest for secondary students. The questions reward physical reasoning over recall — they rarely resemble anything sitting at the end of a textbook chapter.

I found out about the exam while searching for extra work I could do to deepen my understanding of physics. The questions were quite different from the ones in class: they combined physics with fun, and that resonated with me. So I asked my physics teacher to host the exam at our school, which he thankfully accepted.

Afterwards, the students who volunteered to take it played 3v3 basketball with our physics teacher. That is one of the most memorable experiences I have had recently.

Entry 2.3 · Grades 9, 10 and 11 · Distinction in all three

Waterloo Mathematics Contests: Pascal, Cayley, Fermat

University of Waterloo, CEMC. Pascal in Grade 9, Cayley in Grade 10, Fermat in Grade 11. Distinction in all three.

I enjoy mathematics for the same reason I enjoy physics: it is the most elegant way I know of saying something exactly.

Waterloo sets one contest per year group, so these three are the same ladder climbed three years running, each rung harder than the last. As three separate lines on a résumé the progression disappears.

Fermat, in Grade 11, was optional. I took it anyway, in the middle of everything else that year.

Entry 2.4 · Grade 11 · Top 25%

AMC 12

Mathematical Association of America. AMC 12A, November 2025. Top 25%.

Twenty-five questions in seventy-five minutes, scored so that leaving a question blank beats guessing it.

This was one of the most challenging experiences I have had in mathematics. I had a lot of difficulty preparing, especially in geometry, which I am weak at. I did not achieve the highest result here, but it taught me mathematics and how to act when I am confronted with a problem I cannot immediately solve.

Entry 2.5 · Grade 10 · Bronze, Individual round

World Mathematics Team Competition

Held in Incheon. Individual, relay and team rounds. Bronze in the individual round.

A team-based mathematics competition with individual, relay and team rounds. Students interested in mathematics were invited from our grade, and I was lucky enough to join the team with my friends.

It taught me that problem solving does not have to happen alone, and that it benefits everyone involved. That realisation shaped how I approached mathematics and physics afterwards — looking for and forming communities of people interested in the same things.

Entry 2.6 · Grade 10 · International Round Qualifier · 7th nationally

Math League

Qualified for the International Round. Seventh place nationally.

Only the individual round counted towards moving on, but the competition also had group rounds. With a different group of friends from the team I went to WMTC with, I met new people and worked alongside them.

Solving problems together let me think from perspectives I would not have reached alone, which is an experience I would like more of in other areas too.

Entry 2.7 · Grade 9 — present · Second place in Grade 9, organising member since

Chadwick International Mathematics Competition

Chadwick International. Silver Award, March 2024. Now writes problems and supervises exams.

The competition was led by a sophomore I knew from junior varsity volleyball when I was a freshman. I applied as a student who liked mathematics, and I placed second in the high school division.

The leader then asked if I wanted to be an organising member and write problems for the semi-annual middle and high school competition, and I accepted. I now create original problems and supervise the exams.

Making problems instead of solving them made me think more deeply and understand how a problem actually works, which helped my creative thinking and my problem solving.

Entry 2.8 · Grade 10 · Recognition Award

Korea Science & Engineering Fair

KSEF. Korea's national science fair. Recognition Award.

I was not sure which career I would pursue at this point, though I knew I liked physics from the bottom of my heart.

My friends invited me onto a coding project that used artificial intelligence to detect drunk driving from the motion of the car and the movement of the driver’s head.

Entry 2.9 · Grade 9 · Participant

Business Black Box, Wharton Korea Undergraduate Business Society

Wharton Korea Undergraduate Business Society. Participant.

My friends who are interested in economics and business invited me onto their team. Along with my friend from the mathematics team, we were assigned the calculations behind the team’s ideas.

It was interesting to come together with a single goal when everyone had a different background — business, psychology, physics.

The programme ran under Wharton and UPenn undergraduate mentors. We worked real corporate cases — market entry, consumer engagement — under time pressure, synthesising raw data into pitch decks and using frameworks like SWOT, PESTEL and the 4Ps.
